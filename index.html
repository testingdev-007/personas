Copilot + Personas

Use the same "draft, then let a persona review it" process you use with Claude, but inside Word, Outlook, Excel and Teams.

Everything here is illustrative. Names, figures and Copilot responses are invented and simulated in your browser. Nothing on this page calls a real Copilot.

The process in one loop

A plain prompt gets you a competent, generic answer. A persona prompt gets you the answer of the person who will actually read, judge or act on your work.

Step 1

Draft or gather

Write the letter, build the table, open the thread. Copilot can help, but you own the first pass.

Step 2

Review through a persona

Ask Copilot to become the reader: the upset customer, the Head of Ops, the auditor. Ask what they'd challenge, quoting the text.

Step 3

Revise, then verify

Fix what the persona surfaced. Then check figures, wording and policy against your source files. You're accountable for it.

What's the point?

  • "Make it better" has no target. Copilot guesses that better means smoother. A persona defines better as "Ms Patel understands what she's getting".
  • You get a rehearsal, not a rewrite. The most valuable output is the list of objections, before the real person raises them.
  • Several lenses, one document. Customer, regulator and QA each catch different things. You can run them in turn on the same file.
  • Copilot works on your files. That grounding (case notes, packs, threads) is what makes a persona review specific rather than vague.

Running personas together

One prompt, several personas — but that means different things depending on what you're testing. A panel simulates a small mixed group reacting to one piece of work, so you see where it lands unevenly. Independent takes ask several personas to review the same material without letting their answers blend, so you get clean, separate verdicts rather than one committee opinion. A segmented room is the version of a panel that scales past a handful of people — for a real audience of 20, 30, 100, you stop naming individuals and instead describe the groups they fall into.

Panel / audience

Test a presentation or activity against a mixed room

Use this when the group reaction is the answer: will this session, deck or activity work for everyone in the room at once, and where does it lose people.

Watch for: after 3–4 exchanges the personas start sounding the same. Keep it to 4–5 personas, and if the answers start blurring together, re-state who's who partway through.

Independent takes

Get several separate verdicts on one document

Use this when you want clean, uncontaminated feedback — like three separate reviewers, not one meeting where the loudest view wins.

Watch for: ask explicitly for separate, labelled sections, and tell it the personas shouldn't reference or agree with each other — otherwise it quietly merges them into a summary.

Segmented room

Simulate a session for 30, 50 or 100 attendees

Use this when there are too many people to name individually, but you know roughly who's in the room, what they need, and what "worked" would look like.

Watch for: the percentages it gives back are Copilot's reasoning from the segment descriptions you supplied, not a survey of your actual attendees. It's only as good as how accurately you've described the split — treat it as a rehearsal of your assumptions, and check it against real feedback (even a two-question exit poll) afterwards.

You're running a 20-minute live walkthrough of the new triage procedure on Monday, for two new starters plus their manager, who's sitting in to check it lands. Same slides — you want to know how it plays out before you're in the room.

Panel prompt and result

Simulated panel reaction
  • Slide 2, "What is triage?": new starter following closely. Transferee already knows this — candidate to skip or skim. Manager: pace is fine so far.
  • Slide 5, DISP definitions: new starter lost — too many terms in one slide. Transferee fine. Manager: "this is the slide people always stop me on."
  • Slide 8, escalation flowchart: transferee checked out, seen it before elsewhere. New starter needs it slower. Manager: this slide alone is eating a third of the 20 minutes.

Combined verdict: it doesn't hold together as one session for all three — the transferee is disengaged for a third of it on exactly the minutes the new starter needs slowed down. Split it: 10 minutes of core process for everyone, then a separate 10-minute "what's different here" add-on just for transfers.

Independent prompt and result

Simulated independent takes

New starter: I'd still be unsure what happens with a case that has no clear owner — slide 6 assumes I already know who to ask. I wouldn't cut anything; I needed all of it.

Transferee: Slides 2–4 are things I already do under a different name in my old process — cut those to one "what's different" slide. I'd want more on your specific escalation SLAs, which aren't in here at all.

Manager: I'd cut the walkthrough of the case-system screens — that works better as a follow-up 1:1 — and use the time saved to check understanding with a live example.

The difference: the panel told you the session doesn't work as one-size-fits-all and how to restructure it. The independent takes told you what's actually missing for each person — SLAs for the transferee, ownership rules for the new starter, screens vs a live example for the manager. Same 20 minutes of material, two different kinds of answer, and you likely want both.

Worked example: the same walkthrough, scaled to 30 people

The version above works for 3 named people. Now it's the quarterly induction day: 30 attendees for a 45-minute session, and you don't know them individually — but you do know the mix, and you know what "it worked" means: everyone should leave able to log and triage a straightforward case unsupervised.

What you know about the room: roughly 18 complete beginners (new joiners), 8 experienced handlers transferring from another team, 4 team leads sitting in to quality-check the session.

Simulated segmented reaction
  • Beginners (~60% of the room): engaged through slides 1–4, start losing the thread by slide 8 where the flowchart jumps to edge cases before the basics are secure. By the end, most can log a case, but not confidently triage one alone.
  • Transfers (~27%): only engaged during the "what's different here" moments — call it 10 of the 45 minutes. Otherwise report checked out.
  • Team leads (~13%): engaged throughout, but flag that a walkthrough alone won't get beginners to unsupervised triage — they want a practice case built in before the room disperses.

Combined verdict: against the stated goal, roughly 60% of the room won't reliably meet "triage unsupervised" from the walkthrough alone. Add a 10-minute guided practice case before the session ends, and move the transfer-specific content to pre-reading so it stops eating room time for the majority it isn't for.

What changed from the 3-person version: the personas became weighted segments, and the output became a proportion of the room plus a check against your stated goal, rather than three individual voices. The technique is the same rehearsal; the unit changed from a person to a slice of the room.
